I've just finished two days at LDX3 London 2025: by LeadDev, a conference aimed at Tech Leaders (think more Engineering Managers -- EMs -- rather than Individual Contributors -- ICs). 

The agenda was well-suited for Starling. Since I joined five years ago, I've seen us shift from "Start Up" to "Scale Up." LeadDev divided their stages into three main tracks:

* Organisational Leadership
* Technical Strategy
* Ways of Working

The talks and water-cooler conversations confirmed what we've known for a while: we're truly in the AI era. Every stall featured companies showcasing their AI integrations, and about half the talks focused on AI advice and pitfalls, from self-hosting to model tuning.

The conference was a bubble of AI. You'd think that AI is already as essential as WiFi, or already as embedded in our processes as PR reviews. But back in reality, companies are still cautiously exploring. We're still very early in the AI era. Pioneers have taken us to new lands, and Settlers are now slowly embedding this into our Town as we build. This analogy is stolen from the conference itself. 

Companies are tentatively introducing AI into in-house back-office flows first. It's important to try AI on for size with yourself first, before imposing it on your customers. But make no mistake: it's the direction that *everyone* is heading. 

Ben Seymour of Vercel described how similar it feels right now to the introduction of the world's first web page in 1991 by Tim Berners-Lee at CERN. ChatGPT swept over the world with over one hundred million active users in its first three months. Almost overnight, AI has become a companion nearly as familiar as "Googling" something.

But focussing on things not-AI, which probably removes about 50% of the discussion at LDX3, there were more useful insights. Lara Hogan offered a post-pandemic reality check, reminding us that management has traditionally been about providing clear direction. There was a golden era of Engineering Management where we all enjoyed providing Empowerment, but with layoffs and return-to-office mandates, we now need to balance empowerment with direction. At times, our companies will need us to be more directive, which will be a challenge to navigate. 

Stacey Nutbrown shared tips for leading neurodiverse teams, emphasizing that it's not about having all the answers, but about being brave enough to start conversations. Be clear about your needs as a manager, listen to feedback, acknowledge feelings, and most importantly, work together toward a resolution.

Cate Huston resonated with many by acknowledging that, as an EM, your manager may not fully understand your day-to-day work or manage you closely. She outlined self-management techniques and stressed the importance of developing a strategy to provide value to your company.

Alex Karp placed a rather large and emphatic "Do Not Enter" sign on the "Performance Curve" door, referring to the classic 20/70/10 model (20% 'A Team' performers, 70% 'B Team' supporters, and 10% 'C Team' as in "See ya!"). 

I enjoyed a look at what Incident.io have produced to revolutionise On-Call and Incident management, including their own early forray into AI with insights into past incidents. I also had a fascinating conversation with an engineer from PicNic, who are also experiencing the familiar pains of scaling-up. Unblocked presented an AI-driven agent that consolidates information from Confluence and Slack, offering an interactive resource that would be a valuable asset for large companies with exponentially growing dumps of documentation.

LDX3 has hammered home that AI is here already and it's not going away. Even a conference focussed on Engineering Management couldn't escape the unrelenting hype. For managers, there's a warning that the times they are a-changin', but the industry is significantly more prepared for it now than it was decades ago. There are increasing resources tailored specifically for EMs, as well as LeadDev itself.
