Today marks my five-year anniversary at Starling.

It certainly doesn't feel like five years. But when you stop to think back, it does all kind of add up. Every year has been different: the challenges change from personal, to company, to system, to product, to scaling, to sector. I've changed a lot in that time. So has my role. So has the company.

It definitely got off to a rocky start. Three months into my exciting new job, the world hit February/March 2020. Hard. Thinking of that time will send a shiver down many people's spines. It certainly does mine. What followed was the weirdest (and often) darkest time that any of us have/will ever experience. Months, perhaps years. It was hard to really say where it ended. COVID-19 (the world's scariest JIRA ticket) changed everything.

For me I made good of it for a long while. I got very busy at work. It was a new job, and I was eager to succceed. I made a great habit of my working days. Up early, logged on and churning through some code. Ready for standup with PRs to review. As soon as standup was done, a quick 5km run whilst there was a bit of a gap in the day. Back in time to wrap up a few more things before lunch. Sometimes working extra in the evening - it was easy to do, no one had any plans and the laptop was right there.

I think this was, and maybe is, the secret to success early on in a company/career. It certainly worked for me. There's no substitute for cutting your teeth. The aim of the game is to get very busy writing and changing code. I made it my target to never not have something in progress. Any spare minute or gap in the day, there must be something I can do to our code to make it better.

Fast-forward to now. Projects, teams, groups, domains have all flown by. Loads of really special people along the way. If you'd told me in my first year at Starling that I'd be here for five, I wouldn't have been surprised. It's a lovely place to work.

It has challenges. Requirements are thin on the ground. You work almost always in obscurity. If you're not comfortable making judgement calls, or setting of a system with no destination but just a direction, it's going to be a real struggle.

But the people: they're talented, they're kind, they're responsible, and they really put their heart into their work. I admire the hell out of them. It's a privilege to work with them. I'd really like to think I've met some people now that, through one means or another, I'll know for the rest of my life.

Would I still be at Starling in five more years? Not sure. But I definitely want to stay working with these people.
